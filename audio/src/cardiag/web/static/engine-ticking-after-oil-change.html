How Carithm Analyzes an Engine Ticking Sound

Typing "my engine is ticking after an oil change" throws away most of the information that's actually useful for telling causes apart. The recording itself carries that information — Carithm's job is to pull it out and weigh it against the context you provide.

Recording→ Acoustic characteristics→ Pattern analysis→ Possible causes→ Probability-ranked hypotheses→ Recommended next checks

A few of the characteristics that actually matter in a recording, and why a written description tends to lose them:

  • Timing and periodicity — whether the tick is evenly spaced (often tied to a rotating component) or irregular, and how fast that rhythm is.
  • Frequency characteristics — the pitch and tonal quality of the sound, which differs between, say, a light valvetrain tick and a heavier mechanical knock.
  • Continuous vs. transient — whether it's a brief event at startup that fades, or a sound that keeps going once the engine is running.
  • Behavior with RPM — whether the tick speeds up, slows down, or stays constant as engine speed changes.
  • Startup vs. warm-engine behavior — cold-start ticks and warm-engine ticks point toward different mechanical explanations.
  • Context you supply — how long it's been since the service, the oil level reading, and whether you've seen a leak all sharpen the ranking alongside the audio itself.

Carithm weighs these characteristics against patterns associated with different causes and returns them as a ranked list of possibilities rather than a single verdict — because more than one explanation can be consistent with the same sound.

Walk Through It Step by Step

If you'd rather reason through it manually first, work through these in order.

Did the tick start at the very next startup, or develop over the following days?
Immediate, first startup — still confirm the oil level before assuming it's benign Developed over subsequent days — more concerning, check for a slow leak or wrong oil weight
Is the oil level on the dipstick correct, low, or overfilled?
Low — underfill or a leak, top up carefully and recheck promptly Correct — less likely a volume issue, consider priming or viscosity instead Overfilled — can cause foaming or pressure issues, needs draining to spec
Any oil spots, drips, or wet residue under the engine since the service?
Yes — check the drain plug and filter immediately, treat as urgent No — narrow toward viscosity or filter-priming instead
Does the oil pressure gauge or warning light show anything unusual?
Light on or gauge low — stop driving and check before continuing Normal — lower urgency, but still worth resolving the underlying cause

What the Audio Can and Cannot Tell You

Being upfront about the limits of audio analysis is part of what makes it useful. Here's the honest split.

What Carithm can help identify

  • Acoustic patterns in the recording — timing, frequency, and RPM behavior
  • Likely categories of mechanical cause consistent with those patterns
  • A probability-ranked set of possibilities, not a single guess
  • Patterns worth investigating further, based on the sound alone
  • Recommended next checks to narrow things down physically

What audio alone cannot confirm

  • Your actual oil pressure reading
  • Whether there's a physical leak underneath the car
  • Internal mechanical wear or damage
  • The exact failed component
  • A guaranteed diagnosis of any kind

Treat Carithm as a triage step that tells you what's worth checking and roughly how urgently — not a replacement for opening the hood, checking the dipstick, or having a mechanic look at the car when the situation calls for it.

What Should I Check First?

Work through these before assuming the worst.

  1. Check the dipstick on level ground, engine off for a few minutes, to confirm the level is at or near full.
  2. Look under the car (once cool, or carefully) for fresh oil near the drain plug or filter.
  3. Confirm which oil weight was actually used versus what's specified for your engine.
  4. Watch the oil pressure gauge or warning light at idle and while driving.
  5. Smell for burning oil, which can indicate a drip landing on a hot exhaust component.
  6. If it was a DIY change, double-check the filter was pre-filled with oil and hand-tightened, and that the drain plug is torqued to spec.

Have a check-engine code? Note any stored code(s) before clearing anything. Including the code with your recording gives Carithm more context and a more specific ranking.

How to Record the Best Engine Sound

The analysis is only as good as the recording. A few minutes of care here makes a real difference to the result.

  • Find a quiet spot. Background noise, other traffic, or a garage door closing nearby can mask the sound Carithm needs to hear.
  • Shield the mic from wind. Even light wind across the microphone can drown out a quiet tick — record with the hood up in a still area if you can.
  • Keep your phone at a safe distance from any moving components. Never reach into the engine bay while it's running to get a "better" recording.
  • Aim for 10–30 seconds where practical, so the pattern has time to show up clearly.
  • Record at idle first. This is the most useful baseline for comparison.
  • If it's safe to do so, add a brief RPM change — a few seconds of light acceleration in park or neutral — since whether the tick changes with RPM is a meaningful clue.
  • Note whether the engine is cold or warm at the time of the recording.
  • Note when the noise first started relative to the oil change, and whether it changes with RPM, in the context field.

Can You Drive With This Noise?

Ticking after an oil change spans the full range from harmless to genuinely urgent — checking the oil level and looking for a leak are the two most important first steps before deciding whether to keep driving.

Generally lower concern

  • Oil level correct, tick fades within a minute at each startup
  • No visible leaks anywhere under the car
  • No oil pressure warning light
  • Noise not worsening since the first startup after the service

Stop driving and investigate promptly

  • Any visible oil leak since the service
  • Oil pressure warning light on
  • Dipstick reads low
  • Burning oil smell, or a tick that's getting worse rather than better

This is diagnostic guidance, not a safety guarantee — when in doubt, have it looked at rather than assuming it's fine.

Questions About Ticking After an Oil Change

Can AI really identify an engine problem from sound?

Carithm AI analyzes the acoustic characteristics of a recording — timing, frequency pattern, and how the sound behaves at different RPMs — and returns a probability-ranked list of the mechanical causes that pattern is most consistent with. It's a diagnostic aid based on audio patterns, not a certified inspection, and it doesn't replace one.

How should I record an engine ticking noise for Carithm?

Record in as quiet a location as possible, avoid wind hitting the microphone, and keep the phone a safe distance from any moving components. Capture 10–30 seconds at idle, and if it's safe to do so, a few seconds of a light RPM change. Note whether the engine was cold or warm and when the noise first started.

Can a ticking sound have more than one possible cause?

Yes. A single ticking sound after an oil change can be consistent with more than one cause — a brief startup tick and an underfill can sound similar, for instance. That's why Carithm returns a ranked list of possibilities rather than a single answer, and why context like oil level and timing matters.

Can Carithm tell whether I should stop driving?

Carithm can flag when a sound pattern is more consistent with a higher-urgency cause, but it can't confirm actual oil pressure or a physical leak from audio alone. If you see an oil pressure warning light, a visible leak, or a burning oil smell, treat that as urgent regardless of what the audio analysis suggests.

What information should I provide with the recording?

Useful context includes how many days or startups it's been since the oil change, whether the oil level checked out on the dipstick, whether you've seen any oil spots underneath the car, and whether the tick changes with RPM. This helps narrow the ranked list of possibilities alongside the audio itself.
