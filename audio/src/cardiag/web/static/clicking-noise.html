Quick Answer: Why Is My Car Making a Clicking Noise?

The most common reasons a car makes a clicking noise are:

  • Clicking when turning: worn CV joint or axle
  • Clicking from engine: lifters, injectors, low oil, or valve train issues
  • Clicking while driving: loose components, brakes, or suspension parts
  • Clicking when accelerating: CV axle or drivetrain issues
Clicking Location Most Likely Cause Urgency
Front wheels when turning CV joint / CV axle High
Engine bay at idle Fuel injectors, lifters, low oil Medium
Clicking increases with speed Wheel bearing, brakes, rotating component Medium
Clicking after shutdown Heat shields or exhaust cooling Low

Car Clicking Noise Scenarios

Clicking Noise When Turning Left or Right

A clicking noise while turning is one of the most common signs of a worn CV joint. The sound usually becomes louder during sharp turns because the damaged joint has to operate at a larger angle.

  • Repeated clicking during slow turns
  • Louder when accelerating through a turn
  • Often comes from the front wheels

Car Clicking Noise While Driving

A clicking noise while driving can come from rotating parts such as CV joints, wheel components, brakes, or loose hardware. If the clicking speed increases with vehicle speed, the problem is usually related to a rotating component.

Car Clicking Noise When Accelerating

Possible drivetrain, axle, or engine-related issue. Clicking while accelerating can be caused by CV joints, exhaust shields, or engine components laboring under load.

Car Clicking Noise At Idle

Often related to injectors, lifters, or engine components. An engine clicking noise may come from valve lifters, fuel injectors (which is normal but can get loud), low oil level, or other internal components.

Car Clicking Noise But No Warning Light

Many mechanical problems do not trigger dashboard lights. Issues like a worn CV joint, failing lifters, or loose brake hardware will present physically and audibly long before a sensor throws a code.

How Much Does It Cost To Fix a Clicking Noise?

Repair cost depends on the cause. A simple brake clip or loose component may cost little, while replacing a CV axle or engine components can cost significantly more.

Likely Cause Typical Repair Cost Range
CV Axle Replacement $250 – $600
Valve Lifter Replacement $500 – $1,500+

Questions People Ask About Clicking Noises

What does a clicking noise in a car mean?

A clicking noise in a car usually means a component is moving, rotating, or contacting another part. Common causes include CV joints, engine lifters, injectors, brakes, and loose hardware.

Why is my car making a clicking noise when driving?

A clicking noise when driving can stem from worn CV joints, engine valve lifters, loose brake components, or fuel injectors depending on driving speed and throttle application.

How much does it cost to fix a clicking noise in a car?

Repair costs range from under $50 for minor hardware fixes to $250–$600 for a CV axle replacement, or over $1,500 for complex internal engine repairs like valve lifters.

Can I drive with a clicking noise?

Short distance driving may be safe depending on the root cause, but unresolved mechanical clicks—especially from CV joints or brakes—can worsen quickly and trigger severe failure while driving.

Why does my car click when turning left or right?

Clicking while turning is commonly caused by a worn CV joint, especially if the sound increases when the steering wheel is turned sharply.

Why is my car clicking but there is no warning light?

Many mechanical issues such as CV joints, injectors, and lifters do not trigger dashboard warning lights.

Is clicking noise from the engine normal?

Some clicking sounds, such as fuel injectors, are normal. Louder ticking that changes with RPM may require inspection.

Why is my car clicking while driving slowly?

This is often related to braking components, a loose hubcap, or the early stages of CV joint wear that becomes more noticeable at lower speeds.

Why does my car click after I turn it off?

This is typically a normal sound caused by hot metal components, like the exhaust system and heat shields, contracting as they cool down.
