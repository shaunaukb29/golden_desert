What the AI damage detection can actually tell us

Depending on how clear the shot is, the tool can usually pick out things like:

  • Dents and dings — from a shopping cart tap to a proper hail-damaged hood.
  • Scratches, key marks, and scuffs — including paint transfer and clear-coat damage.
  • Cracked or chipped glass — the kind of chip that's cheap now and expensive in six weeks.
  • Broken light housings — cracked lenses, hanging brackets, that sort of thing.
  • Cracked bumpers and bent panels — your standard fender-bender damage.
  • Wheel and tire damage — flats, curbed rims, obvious bends.

That's the visible stuff. The less visible stuff is where things get more interesting — and it's worth reading before you trust any number, including ours.

What a photo can't tell you

Here's the annoying part about auto body damage: a lot of the cost isn't in what you can see. A bumper can look like a $300 scuff and buff job and turn into $1,200 once the shop pulls it off and finds a cracked mounting bracket or a parking sensor that's no longer sitting where it should.

Things a photo genuinely cannot show you:

  • Bent structural components behind the visible panel
  • Broken mounting brackets or clips
  • Parking sensors or cameras built into a damaged bumper
  • Whether ADAS calibration will be needed after the repair
  • Damage to the bumper's internal foam or reinforcement bar
  • Mechanical or alignment issues from the same impact

None of that means the estimate is useless, it just means it's a floor, not a ceiling. If the shop's quote comes in higher than what you saw here, that's not automatically a red flag. It might just mean they found something the camera couldn't.

Getting a photo that actually works

The estimate is only as good as the photo, so a few things help a lot:

  • Shoot in daylight or good indoor lighting, hard shadows hide damage and confuse the read.
  • Get the whole damaged area in frame, not just the worst-looking inch of it.
  • Take one close-up of the damage and one wider shot showing where it sits on the car.
  • Keep it in focus, a blurry photo of a bad dent reads worse than a sharp photo of a bad dent.
  • Don't crop out the surrounding panel; context matters for judging severity.
  • If the damage wraps around a corner or spans two panels, take a couple of angles.

Why people actually use this

Someone hit my car in a parking lot

You want to know roughly what you're looking at before you decide whether it's worth chasing down insurance info, filing a claim, or just paying for it yourself. If your bumper has a scrape near a sensor cluster or camera housing, budget for the possibility that it's more than a scuff and buff.

A rough estimate is a decent gut-check here, not a way to argue the shop down. If the numbers are in the same neighborhood, that's a reasonable sign the quote is fair. If they're wildly apart, it's worth asking the shop directly what's driving the difference is usually it's hidden damage, paint blending, or parts they priced OEM instead of aftermarket.

I'm buying a used car

This is where I'd be more cautious. A photo estimate can flag obviously repaired panels — mismatched paint, uneven gaps, overspray, but it can't prove prior collision history on its own. I'm returning a rental

Car Repair Cost Estimates: Why Two Similar-Looking Dents Cost Differently

A dent is not a dent. Two cars can roll into the shop with what looks like the same ding and walk out with very different bills, depending on things like:

  • Paintless dent repair vs. conventional repair — if the paint isn't cracked, PDR is usually far cheaper.
  • Panel replacement vs. repair — sometimes it's genuinely cheaper to replace a panel than fix it.
  • Aluminum vs. steel — aluminum panels generally cost more to repair.
  • Paint blending — matching a modern metallic or pearl finish often means blending paint into panels next to the damage, not just the damaged one.
  • OEM vs. aftermarket parts — original manufacturer parts cost more than aftermarket equivalents.
  • Sensors and cameras — a bumper hiding a parking sensor or camera adds recalibration cost on top of the bodywork.
  • Local labor rates — shop rates vary a lot by region, and that alone can shift a quote by hundreds of dollars.

With all that in mind, here are rough US ballparks for common damage — treat these as a general sense of scale, not a quote:

  • Minor dent (paintless repair): roughly $75–$250
  • Dent with cracked or chipped paint: roughly $300–$700
  • Deep scratch or key mark: roughly $150–$900, depending on panel size
  • Cracked bumper, repair or replace: roughly $200–$1,500
  • Headlight or taillight replacement: roughly $150–$800 including labor
  • Windshield chip repair: roughly $60–$400; full replacement often $250–$1,000+
  • Flat tire or curbed rim: roughly $20–$150 for a flat, $100–$500+ if the rim is bent

Reading your estimate the right way

If the estimate comes back low, that's good news, but don't treat it as a guarantee — it's a reflection of what the photo showed, not a certified number. If it comes back higher than you expected, don't panic either; ranges are wide on purpose because real repair costs vary by shop, region, and what's hiding behind the panel.

Where this is genuinely useful: deciding whether damage looks minor enough to skip a claim, sanity-checking a shop's quote, documenting a rental before drop off, or getting a first read on a used car's bodywork. Where it's not a substitute: anything involving an actual insurance claim, a legal dispute, or a decision to buy a car sight-unseen.

When to just get it looked at in person

Some situations are worth skipping the estimate and going straight to a shop or mechanic:

  • The damage is near a sensor, camera, or badge cluster on the bumper or grille.
  • The car pulls to one side, or anything feels different after the impact.
  • There's a warning light on the dash that wasn't there before.
  • You're buying the car and the seller can't explain a repaired-looking panel.
  • The damage is anywhere near a wheel well, suspension component, or the frame rail.

None of that is something a photo can settle — it just tells you where to point a professional.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is this car damage estimator tool actually free?

Yes — no account, no email, no catch. Upload a photo and get a rough repair range with nothing to sign up for.

Can a photo actually tell me how much my repair will cost?

It can give you a reasonable range based on what's visible. It can't account for hidden damage, so treat it as a floor rather than a final number.

Can the AI see damage underneath the bumper?

No. It only reads what's in the photo. Anything behind the plastic brackets, sensors, structural components is invisible to it, same as it would be to you looking at the car.

Why is my estimate different from the body shop's quote?

Usually it's hidden damage the shop found once they got hands on, or a difference in parts pricing (OEM vs. aftermarket) and local labor rates that a photo can't factor in.

Should I repair the dent or replace the panel?

That depends on the panel's condition, material, and how the paint is holding up — it's a call best made by a shop that can see the damage in person, not from a photo alone.

Can I use this before filing an insurance claim?

You can use it to get a sense of whether the damage is worth claiming versus paying out of pocket. It's not something you can submit to an insurer as an appraisal.

Can I use this to check a used car before buying?

It can flag obvious signs like mismatched paint or an odd panel gap. It can't confirm or rule out prior accident history — pair it with a vehicle history report and, ideally, an in-person look.

What if I only have one photo?

One clear photo is usually enough for a rough read. Multiple angles just help if the damage spans more than one panel or wraps around an edge.

Why does a small-looking dent sometimes cost so much?

Usually paint. If the clear coat is cracked or the color needs blending into neighboring panels to match, that can cost more than fixing the dent itself.

Is the problem something you can hear instead of see?
