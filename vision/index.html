What AI car damage detection can spot in a photo

Depending on how clear the shot is, the tool can usually pick out:

  • Dents and dings: from a shopping cart tap to a proper hail-damaged hood.
  • Scratches, key marks, and scuffs: including paint transfer and clear-coat damage.
  • Cracked or chipped glass: the kind of windshield chip that's cheap now and expensive in six weeks.
  • Broken headlights and taillights: cracked lenses, hanging brackets, that sort of thing.
  • Cracked bumpers: your standard fender-bender damage.
  • Bent or creased body panels: doors, fenders, hoods, and quarter panels.
  • Wheel and tire damage: flats, curbed rims, obvious bends.

That's the visible stuff. The less visible stuff is where things get more interesting, and it's worth reading before you trust any number, including ours.

Why two similar-looking dents cost differently

A dent is not a dent. Two cars can roll into the shop with what looks like the same ding and walk out with very different bills, depending on:

  • Paintless dent repair vs. conventional repair: if the paint isn't cracked, PDR is usually far cheaper.
  • Panel replacement vs. repair: sometimes it's genuinely cheaper to replace a panel than fix it.
  • Aluminum vs. steel: aluminum panels generally cost more to repair.
  • Paint blending: matching a modern metallic or pearl finish often means blending paint into the panels next to the damage, not just the damaged one.
  • OEM vs. aftermarket parts: original manufacturer parts cost more than aftermarket equivalents.
  • Sensors and cameras: a bumper hiding a parking sensor or camera adds recalibration cost on top of the bodywork.
  • Local labor rates: shop rates vary a lot by region, and that alone can shift a quote by hundreds of dollars.

What a photo can't tell you

Here's the annoying part about auto body damage: a lot of the cost isn't in what you can see. A bumper can look like a $300 scuff-and-buff and turn into $1,200 once the shop pulls it off and finds a cracked mounting bracket or a parking sensor that's no longer sitting where it should.

Things a photo genuinely cannot show you:

  • Bent structural components behind the visible panel
  • Broken mounting brackets or clips
  • Parking sensors or cameras built into a damaged bumper
  • Whether ADAS calibration will be needed after the repair
  • Damage to the bumper's internal foam or reinforcement bar
  • Mechanical or alignment issues from the same impact

None of that makes the estimate useless. It just means it's a floor, not a ceiling. If the shop's quote comes in higher than what you saw here, that's not automatically a red flag. It might mean they found something the camera couldn't.

How to take a car damage photo the AI can read

The estimate is only as good as the photo, so a few things help a lot:

  • Shoot in daylight or good indoor lighting. Hard shadows and glare hide damage and confuse the read.
  • Get the whole damaged area in frame, not just the worst-looking inch of it.
  • Take one close-up of the damage and one wider shot showing where it sits on the car.
  • Keep it in focus. A blurry photo of a bad dent reads worse than a sharp photo of a bad dent.
  • Don't crop out the surrounding panel. Context matters for judging severity.
  • If the damage wraps around a corner or spans two panels, take a couple of angles.

When people use an AI car damage checker

Someone hit my car in a parking lot

You want to know roughly what you're looking at before you decide whether it's worth chasing down insurance info, filing a claim, or just paying for it yourself. The estimate gives you something to work from. It won't tell you who's at fault or settle a dispute. That's still a conversation with insurance or the other driver.

I scraped my bumper

Bumper scrapes are deceptive. The plastic itself is often the cheap part. The clips, brackets, and anything mounted behind it (sensors, cameras) can quietly add hundreds of dollars. If your scrape is near a sensor cluster or camera housing, budget for the possibility that it's more than a scuff-and-buff.

Checking whether a body shop's repair quote is fair

Run a photo of the damage through the estimator and compare the range to the quote. If the numbers are in the same neighborhood, that's a reasonable sign the quote is fair. If they're far apart, ask the shop what's driving the difference. It's usually hidden damage, paint blending, or parts priced OEM instead of aftermarket. An itemized estimate (parts, labor hours, paint, calibration) makes that conversation easy.

I need photos for an insurance claim

Claims generally go smoother with the same handful of shots: one wide shot showing the whole car, a mid-range shot of the full damaged panel, and a close-up of the damage itself. Take them in good light, then run the close-up through the estimator to see whether the damage is anywhere near your deductible before you decide to file.

I'm buying a used car

This is where I'd be more cautious. A photo estimate can flag obviously repaired panels, like mismatched paint, uneven gaps, or overspray, but it can't prove prior collision history on its own. Pull a vehicle history report and, if anything looks off in person, have a body shop or independent mechanic take a real look before you buy.

I'm returning a rental car

Practices vary by company and location, but it's a good habit to photograph the car yourself before you hand back the keys: timestamped, all sides, close-ups of anything questionable. If a damage charge shows up later that you don't recognize, your own documentation puts you in a much better position to dispute it.

Search for AI vehicle damage detection and many of the results are built for insurers, dealerships, and rental fleets, sold by contract or per inspection, often as an API or SDK. Carithm sits at the other end: a free, consumer-facing photo tool for one car, one photo, and one ballpark.

If you run a fleet or a claims operation, you'll want purpose-built inspection software with structured reports and audit trails. If you just want to know whether a dent is worth worrying about, this is the quicker route.

When to just get it looked at in person

Some situations are worth skipping the estimate and going straight to a shop or mechanic:

  • The damage is near a sensor, camera, or badge cluster on the bumper or grille.
  • The car pulls to one side, or anything feels different after the impact.
  • There's a warning light on the dash that wasn't there before.
  • You're buying the car and the seller can't explain a repaired-looking panel.
  • The damage is anywhere near a wheel well, suspension component, or the frame rail.

A photo can't settle any of that. It just tells you where to point a professional.

Frequently asked questions

Is this AI car damage detection tool really free?

Yes. No account, no email, no catch. Upload a photo and get a rough repair range with nothing to sign up for.

Is there an app, or do I need to download anything?

No download needed. Carithm runs in your browser on iPhone, Android, and desktop. On a phone you can pick a photo from your gallery or take a new one with your camera.

Can a photo actually tell me how much my repair will cost?

It can give you a reasonable range based on what's visible. It can't account for hidden damage, so treat it as a floor rather than a final number.

How accurate is AI car damage detection for small dents and scratches?

It depends mostly on the photo. Clear, well-lit, in-focus shots of the whole panel work best. Shallow dings on curved glossy panels, hairline scratches, and dark paint in harsh sun or glare are hard for any camera-based system, and for the human eye too. If a read looks off, retake the photo from another angle in softer light and compare.

Can the AI detect windshield, headlight, and wheel damage?

Yes, when the damage is visible: cracks and chips in glass, cracked or broken light housings, curbed or bent rims, and flat tires. It can't tell whether a windshield chip will spread or whether a replacement will need camera recalibration.

Can the AI see damage underneath the bumper?

No. It only reads what's in the photo. Anything behind the plastic, such as brackets, sensors, and structural components, is invisible to it, same as it would be to you looking at the car.

Why is my estimate different from the body shop's quote?

Usually it's hidden damage the shop found once they got hands on the car, or a difference in parts pricing (OEM vs. aftermarket) and local labor rates that a photo can't factor in.

How can I check whether a body shop's repair quote is fair?

Upload a photo of the damage to get a ballpark, then compare it with the quote. If they're in the same neighborhood, the quote is probably reasonable. If the shop's number is much higher, ask for an itemized estimate covering parts, labor hours, paint and blending, and any sensor recalibration, and consider a second quote.

Should I repair the dent or replace the panel?

That depends on the panel's condition, material, and how the paint is holding up. It's a call best made by a shop that can see the damage in person, not from a photo alone.

Can I use this before filing an insurance claim?

You can use it to get a sense of whether the damage is worth claiming versus paying out of pocket. It's not something you can submit to an insurer as an appraisal.

Should I file an insurance claim for minor damage?

If the repair estimate is close to or below your deductible, paying out of pocket is usually cheaper, and a claim for minor damage can raise your premium for several years. How much depends on your insurer and state, so check your policy before deciding.

Can I use this to check a used car before buying?

It can flag obvious signs like mismatched paint or an odd panel gap. It can't confirm or rule out prior accident history, so pair it with a vehicle history report and, ideally, an in-person look.

Why does a small-looking dent sometimes cost so much?

Usually paint. If the clear coat is cracked or the color needs blending into neighboring panels to match, that can cost more than fixing the dent itself.

What happens if I damage a rental car?

Rental companies bill you for new damage found at return unless it's covered by a damage waiver, your personal auto insurance, or credit card protection. Photographing the car yourself before you return it helps document its condition.

Is the problem something you can hear instead of see?

A ticking engine, grinding brake, whining bearing, or rattling suspension: Carithm has a separate tool that listens to a recording and gives you an idea of what's going on.
